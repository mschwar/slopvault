Thought for 2m 49s
There is no single authoritative percentage for this, because “requires chips” can mean very different things.

A strict lower bound is about 0.8% of world GDP: Deloitte projects 2026 global semiconductor sales at $975 billion, while the IMF puts world nominal GDP at about $123.58 trillion. That works out to 0.79%. 

But that badly understates how much GDP relies on semiconductors. The OECD says semiconductors are a crucial input across ICT/electronics, motor vehicles, machinery, and telecommunications; for ICT/electronics specifically, semiconductor value added is 8% of final demand, versus 2% for primary energy. The OECD also says chip disruptions can affect a broad range of downstream industries, and S&P Global Mobility estimates the 2021 chip shortage cut global light-vehicle production by more than 9.5 million units. 

So the most honest answer is:

Direct semiconductor sales: about 0.8% of world GDP.
GDP that materially relies on semiconductors: much larger, likely a majority of the global economy.

A cautious practical estimate would be well above 50%, but that is an inference, not an official published statistic. One reason is that even the digital economy alone is estimated at 4.5% to 15.5% of global GDP, depending on definition, and chips also underpin large non-digital sectors like autos, machinery, industrial equipment, telecom, logistics, and modern finance.

Thought for 2m 31s
Yes — as a loose heuristic, “over 80%” is plausible. As a hard quantitative claim, it is not safe unless you label it clearly as an inference rather than a measured statistic. I have not found an official global estimate that says “X% of world GDP has a semiconductor somewhere in its supply chain.” 

The reason the heuristic is plausible is that semiconductors are an unusually upstream input. The OECD says semiconductor production is one of the most upstream industries in its input-output database, that chips can pass through numerous intermediary production stages, and that disruptions can affect a multitude of downstream industries that depend on them directly or indirectly. The OECD also describes semiconductors as a crucial input across industries such as ICT/electronics, motor vehicles, machinery, and telecommunications. 

The breadth is visible in the OECD’s value-chain mapping: of semiconductor downstream intermediate-material flows, 42% go to electronics, 5.6% to electrical equipment, and 5.4% to machinery and equipment, while 31.8% remain within the semiconductor industry itself. Separately, the World Bank reports that industry accounted for 25.6% of world GDP in 2024, and UNCTAD says chips and other electronic components power digital trade; it also reports that business e-commerce sales in 43 countries representing about three quarters of global GDP reached $27 trillion in 2022. Taken together, that makes an 80%+ “somewhere upstream” assumption quite believable in practice. 

So I’d phrase it this way: “It is reasonable to infer that well over 80% of global GDP depends at least indirectly on semiconductors somewhere in the supply chain, but that figure is an inference, not an official measured statistic.” That wording is much more defensible.

This is the most important step, because these entries are not all the same kind of thing.

You have at least 4 different types:

Infrastructure: electricity, internet, cellular spectrum

Service/network: telegraphs, telephones, radio, television, social media

Telegrams: messages per 100 people, or % of towns with service

Radio / TV: % of households owning a receiver

Telephones: % of people or households with a telephone line

Internet: % of population using internet

Cellphones: % of population owning or using a cellphone

Social media: % of population using social media monthly

So the rule is:

Normalize around the nearest real user-facing adoption variable

T0-cost viability: first year it became affordable enough for mass adoption

For cross-technology comparison, T0-scale availability is usually best.

## Standard Definitions

### Time Definitions

- **Invention year** = first workable/practical invention or enabling concept used for this project
- **Commercial launch year (T0)** = first U.S. commercial/public launch
- **Delta** = `commercial_launch_year - invention_year`

### U.S. Adoption Milestones

Where applicable, later population milestones will include:

- **T10** = years from T0 to 10% adoption
- **T25** = years from T0 to 25% adoption
- **T50** = years from T0 to 50% adoption
- **T75** = years from T0 to 75% adoption

### Preferred Denominators

Use the most appropriate denominator for each technology:

- **Population**
- **Households**
- **Firms**
- **Population coverage**
- **GDP exposure**

### Notes on Comparability

Not all entries are directly comparable. Some are:

- infrastructures

- components

Accordingly, each entry should be assigned the most defensible adoption metric rather than forced into a single denominator.

## Research Rules for Agents

### 1. Every entry must defend its date choices
Agents should not just provide years. They must explain:

- why the invention year was chosen
- why the U.S. commercial launch year qualifies as T0
- why alternative dates were rejected

### 2. Prefer practical and socially meaningful milestones
For this project, “invention” should usually mean:

- first workable practical invention
- not merely scientific discovery
- not merely theoretical proposal

“T0” should mean:

- first real U.S. commercial/public availability
- not just a lab demo
- not just regulatory discussion unless the item is inherently regulatory/infrastructural

### 3. Metric choice matters
Agents must choose the best adoption metric for the entry’s type:

- household penetration
- population usage
- firm adoption
- coverage
- GDP exposure
- transaction share
- freight share
- sectoral dependence

### 4. Separate direct adoption from indirect dependence
Especially for:

- semiconductors
- integrated circuits
- microprocessors
- GPS
- satellites
- cloud
- AI foundation models

Agents should note whether adoption is:

- direct user adoption
- embedded infrastructure dependence
- sectoral / GDP dependence

### 5. Capture disputes explicitly
Some entries will have date ambiguity. Agents should record:

- candidate date A
- candidate date B
- preferred date
- justification

## Recommended agentic approach

### Phase 1: Definition lock
Before filling data, have the methods agent and QA agent define:

- invention-year rules
- T0 rules
- allowed denominators
- acceptable adoption metrics
- citation standards
- dispute logging format

Output:
- one short standards memo
- one worksheet template
- one example completed entry

### Phase 2: Parallel entry collection
Each domain agent fills only these columns first:

- Technology
- Why it belongs
- Invention year
- U.S. commercial launch (T0)
- Delta
- Recommended U.S. adoption metric
- Denominator
- Notes / definition choices
- Primary sources
- Secondary sources
- Research status

Do **not** collect T10/T25/T50/T75 yet.

That keeps Phase 2 narrow and fast.

### Phase 3: QA adjudication
The QA agent reviews all entries for:

- bad invention definitions
- weak T0 choices
- category drift
- denominator inconsistency
- unsupported date claims
- duplicate or overlapping entries
- entries needing split treatment

### Phase 4: Milestone data collection
Once dates and metrics are locked, assign agents again to fill:

- T10
- T25
- T50
- T75
- peak adoption
- years from 10% to 50%

This phase is much harder than Phase 2 and benefits from a stable scaffold.

### Phase 5: Harmonization
Methods agent standardizes:

- formatting
- citation style
- footnotes
- dispute notes
- final master dataset structure

### Phase 6: Synthesis
Final synthesis agent produces:

- ranked adoption-speed comparisons
- lag from invention to T0
- lag from T0 to mass adoption
- comparisons across domains
- layered-dependency narrative
- candidate visualizations

## Practical management suggestions

### Keep two artifacts

1. **Master scaffold**
   The canonical table.

2. **Entry dossiers**
   One markdown file per technology with notes, sources, disputes, and date logic.

### Require confidence labels

For each entry, agents should mark:

- **High confidence**
- **Medium confidence**
- **Low confidence**

### Require source hierarchy

Agents should prioritize:

1. primary or official historical sources
2. authoritative institutional histories
3. strong secondary syntheses

### Freeze taxonomy early

Do not let agents invent new categories midstream unless flagged for review.
